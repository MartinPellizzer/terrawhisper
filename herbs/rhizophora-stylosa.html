Rhizophora stylosa

What's the taxonomical classification of Rhizophora stylosa?

Rhizophora stylosa belongs to the kingdom Plantae and is classified under the phylum Streptophyta. Within this lineage, it is categorized under the class Equisetopsida and the subclass Magnoliidae. The plant follows the biological order Malpighiales and is a member of the family Rhizophoraceae. Finally, it is placed within the genus Rhizophora, specifically identified by the species epithet stylosa.

What are the morphological characteristics of this plant?

Rhizophora stylosa has a woody structure characterized by prominent stilt roots that emerge from the main trunk and branches to provide stability in soft mud. The leaves are elliptical or oblong, featuring a thick, leathery texture and a bright green upper surface with a paler underside. Its flowering structure consists of small, fragrant white or cream-colored flowers that grow in clusters. The plant produces long, cylindrical woody fruits that contain viviparous seedlings capable of germinating while still attached to the parent tree. These specialized morphological adaptations allow the species to thrive in the unstable, saline environments of intertidal zones.

What is the geographical distribution of this plant?

This plant is native to the tropical and subtropical mangrove ecosystems of the Indo-West Pacific region, stretching from the eastern coast of Africa to the islands of the western Pacific Ocean. It thrives in intertidal zones where it can establish itself in soft, muddy substrates. Its range extends across many coastal areas of Southeast Asia, including Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thailand. The species is also commonly found along the northern and western coasts of Australia. These habitats provide the necessary saline conditions and warm temperatures required for its survival.

How is this plant cultivated?

This plant is cultivated primarily through the direct planting of mature propagules into soft, anaerobic intertidal mudflats during low tide.

Successful establishment requires selecting sites with consistent tidal inundation and appropriate salinity levels to support root development. Growers must monitor the substrate to ensure it provides enough nutrients and stability for the developing cable roots. Once planted, the saplings require minimal intervention aside from protection from excessive herbivory or heavy wave action.

Regular observation of the sediment quality helps maintain the health of the expanding mangrove forest.

According to a study published by "PloS one", the stems of Rhizophora stylosa showed more frequent colonization and species diversity than the roots, leaves, hypocotyls, and flower tissues. The study investigated the antioxidant potential of 46 fungal endophytes derived from this mangrove species. A relatively high proportion of 84.8% of the isolates displayed antioxidant capacity with %RSA > 50%. One specific isolate, HQD-6, produced flufuran as a radical scavenger with IC50 values of 34.85±1.56 and 9.75±0.58 μg/mL. These findings suggest that the endophytic fungal community thriving on Rhizophora stylosa may be a sustainable resource for natural antioxidants.

What traditional systems uses this plant?

This plant, Rhizophora stylosa, has been utilized by coastal communities for centuries to produce tannins for leather tanning and as a source of fuel for cooking and shipbuilding.

The bark is frequently harvested to extract tannins, which serve as a natural preservative for hides and a mordant in traditional dyeing processes. Local populations have historically relied on the dense wood to construct durable tools and household items due to its strength and resistance to decay. In many maritime cultures, the wood is also processed into charcoal, providing a consistent heat source for domestic activities.

These historical practices reflect a deep integration of the species into the survival and craftsmanship of various indigenous societies.
