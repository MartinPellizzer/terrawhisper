Cynara cardunculus

Cardoon

Cynara cardunculus, commonly known as the wild artichoke or cardoon, is a versatile Mediterranean perennial celebrated for its medicinal properties. Rich in bioactive compounds like cynarin, it serves as a potent hepatoprotective agent, supporting liver function, aiding digestion, and exhibiting significant antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects in traditional herbal medicine.

  • Scientific name: Cynara cardunculus
  • Family: Asteraceae
  • Native range: Europe

Taxonomical Classification

This plant belongs to the kingdom Plantae and is classified under the phylum Streptophyta and the class Equisetopsida. Within the subclass Magnoliidae, it follows the order Asterales and is a prominent member of the family Asteraceae. Ultimately, its taxonomic identity is defined by the genus Cynara.

Common Names

This plant, known by various names depending on the region and language, is recognized as the Cardoon in both English-speaking Canada (en-ca) and Great Britain (en-gb). In Germany, it is referred to as Wilde Artischocke (de), while in Azerbaijan, it is called İspaniya ənginarı (az). Additionally, in Catalan, the plant is known as herbacol (ca).

Distribution

This plant exhibits a diverse presence across several regions of the European continent. In Northern Europe, its distribution includes the territories of Great Britain and Ireland. Moving toward the central regions, the species can also be found throughout Middle Europe. Specifically, it is documented to grow within the borders of Austria and Belgium. Furthermore, its range extends across the Middle European landscapes of Czechia and Slovakia.

Plant Parts

This plant, commonly known as the artichoke or cardoon, is a member of the thistle family, and while most of its medicinal value is traditionally derived from the leaves and flower heads, its botanical structure also includes the fruit. The fruit of Cynara cardunculus is a small, dry, one-seeded structure known as an achene, which is produced after the flowering stage. These tiny fruits are typically dispersed by the wind, playing a vital role in the plant's reproductive cycle and the spread of the species across its natural habitats.
