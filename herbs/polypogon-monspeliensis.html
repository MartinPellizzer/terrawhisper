Annual rabbitsfoot grass, scientifically known as Polypogon monspeliensis, is a delicate, tufted annual herb often found in disturbed soils. Renowned for its soft, tail-like seed heads, it holds traditional significance in various cultures. While primarily considered a weed, its unique botanical properties offer intriguing possibilities for medicinal study.

Reproduction

The reproduction of Polypogon monspeliensis is characterized by a sexual process through bisexual flowers, although asexual reproduction is absent. The plant utilizes an abiotic pollination syndrome, specifically relying on the wind for pollination. Its flowering period is concentrated around April, with both the start and end of the flowering season occurring during this month. Once fertilization occurs, the plant produces dry fruits that contain a prolific number of seeds, often exceeding 1,000 seeds per fruit. These seeds are notably small and lightweight, with a mean mass of approximately 0.000989375 g (ranging from a minimum of 1e-05 g to a maximum of 0.00014 g), a mean length of 1.3 mm, and a mean width of 0.6 mm, resulting in a consistent seed volume of 0.082 mm³. Dispersal is achieved through an anemochorous syndrome, meaning the seeds are dispersed by the wind. Notably, self-fertilization is absent in this species, emphasizing its reliance on external mechanisms for successful reproductive cycles.
