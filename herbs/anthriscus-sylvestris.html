Cow parsley, scientifically known as Anthriscus sylvestris, is a delicate perennial herb frequently found in hedgerows and meadows. While celebrated for its lace-like white umbels, it holds traditional roles in herbalism. However, extreme caution is required, as it closely resembles highly toxic plants like hemlock, necessitating expert identification.

Reproduction

The reproduction of Anthriscus sylvestris is characterized by a sexual process involving biotic pollination, specifically through an insect pollination syndrome. The flowering period typically commences in May and extends through July. During this reproductive phase, the plant relies on cross-pollination, as self-fertilization is noted to be absent, and it does not employ asexual reproduction. Following successful pollination, the plant produces fruits that vary in size, with a mean length of 0.75 cm (ranging from 0.5 cm to 1 cm) and a mean width of 0.125 cm (ranging from 0.1 cm to 0.15 cm). The resulting seeds are quite small, possessing a mean mass of approximately 0.00417 g, with individual seed weights ranging from a minimum of 0.00229 g to a maximum of 0.00882 g. Dispersal of the progeny is achieved through an anemochorous syndrome, meaning the seeds are primarily dispersed by the wind.
