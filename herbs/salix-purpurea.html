Purple willow, scientifically known as Salix purpurea, is a versatile shrub prized for both its ornamental beauty and medicinal properties. Historically, its bark has been utilized to treat inflammation and pain, containing salicin—the precursor to aspirin. This resilient plant remains a vital subject in both traditional and modern herbalism.

Reproduction

The reproduction of Salix purpurea is characterized by a sexual process through a dioecious breeding system, meaning individual plants are either male or female, and self-fertilization is notably absent. The flowering period typically begins in March and concludes in April. During this time, the plant utilizes a biotic pollination syndrome, specifically relying on insects for pollen transfer. Following successful pollination, the plant produces fruit in the form of a capsule. These capsules are dehiscent, meaning they split open at maturity to release the seeds. The seeds are extremely lightweight, with a consistent mean, minimum, and maximum mass of 9e-05 g. To facilitate the spread of the species, Salix purpurea employs an anemochorous dispersal syndrome, utilizing the wind to transport its tiny seeds to new locations. There is no recorded evidence of asexual reproduction for this species.
