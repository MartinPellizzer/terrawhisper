African mahogany

Afzelia africana · Accepted scientific name

Scientific literature: Very Sparse (39 studies) · ★☆☆☆☆

African mahogany, scientifically known as Afzelia africanica, is a majestic tropical tree highly valued for its medicinal and timber properties. Indigenous to various African regions, its bark, leaves, and seeds are utilized in traditional medicine to treat ailments ranging from inflammation and skin conditions to gastrointestinal issues and infections.

Distribution

This plant is primarily distributed across various regions within West Tropical Africa. Specifically, its presence is recorded in the nation of Benin. It also grows within the borders of Burkina Faso and Gambia. Furthermore, the species can be found throughout Ghana. Finally, its geographical range extends to Guinea-Bissau.

Physiology

The physiology of Afzelia africana is characterized by its utilization of the C3 photosynthetic pathway, a mechanism common to most woody tropical trees where carbon dioxide fixation occurs primarily through the enzyme RuBisCO within the mesophyll cells. Unlike plants that utilize C4 or CAM pathways to minimize photorespiration, this species relies on standard C3 metabolism to convert light energy into chemical energy, which supports its robust growth in seasonal forest environments. Regarding its nutrient acquisition strategies, Afzelia africana is not a nitrogen fixer, meaning it does not possess specialized root nodules containing symbiotic bacteria for atmospheric nitrogen fixation; instead, it must rely on the uptake of dissolved nitrates and ammonium from the soil through its root system to meet its metabolic nitrogen requirements.

Reproduction

The reproduction of Afzelia africa is characterized by the production of dry, pod-type fruits that serve as the vessel for its seeds. These pods are dehiscent, meaning they split open at maturity to facilitate the release of the seeds. The seeds themselves exhibit a consistent volume, with both the minimum and maximum recorded values, as well as the mean, reaching 6400 mm³. In terms of weight, the seeds vary slightly, with a minimum mass of 2.222 g, a maximum mass of 2.77775 g, and an average seed mass of approximately 2.572783333 g. The dispersal of these seeds follows a zoochorous syndrome, indicating that they are spread via animals, which plays a critical role in the plant's reproductive success and spatial distribution.
