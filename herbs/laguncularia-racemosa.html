White mangrove, scientifically known as Laguncularia racemosa, is a versatile coastal shrub prized in traditional medicine. Often found in saline wetlands, its leaves and bark contain bioactive compounds with significant antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties. This resilient plant offers promising therapeutic potential for treating various skin and systemic ailments.

Physiology

The physiology of Laguuncularia racemosa is characterized by highly specialized adaptations to hypersaline and anaerobic intertidal environments, functioning primarily through complex salt management and specialized gas exchange mechanisms. To survive in waterlogged, oxygen-poor substrates, the plant utilizes pneumatophores—specialized aerial roots that facilitate lenticel-mediated gas exchange to maintain aerobic respiration in the submerged root systems. Its metabolic processes are heavily geared toward osmoregulation, employing salt exclusion and secretion mechanisms to manage high salinity levels in its tissues. Regarding its nutrient cycling capabilities, the plant does not function as a nitrogen fixer; it lacks the symbiotic relationship with diazotrophic bacteria required to convert atmospheric nitrogen into bioavailable forms, meaning its nitrogen acquisition is entirely dependent on the availability of nitrates and ammonium within the surrounding soil or sediment.
