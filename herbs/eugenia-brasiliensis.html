Brazil-cherry

Eugenia brasiliensis · Accepted scientific name

Scientific literature: Very Extensive (240 studies) · ★★★★★

Brazil-cherry, scientifically known as Eugenia brasiliensis, is a versatile evergreen shrub native to South America. Renowned for its antioxidant-rich, edible fruits, it offers significant medicinal potential. This plant is traditionally valued for its anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and digestive benefits, making it a subject of interest in modern ethnopharmacology.

Distribution

This plant exhibits a widespread geographical distribution spanning several distinct tropical regions across the globe. The species also reaches the North-Central Pacific in Hawaii and the Caribbean within Trinidad and Tobago. Finally, a significant portion of its range is located in Brazil, particularly throughout the Northeast region.

The life history of Eugenia brasiliensis is characterized by its status as a perennial species, allowing it to persist in its environment over many growing seasons. As a phanerophyte, its reproductive shoots are elevated well above the ground, a structural adaptation that defines its growth habit. The plant maintains an evergreen deciduousness, ensuring that it retains its foliage throughout the year rather than shedding it seasonally. This combination of a long-lived lifecycle, a phanerophyte life form, and evergreen characteristics contributes to its stable presence within its natural ecological niche.

Physiology

The physiology of Eugenia brasiliensis is characterized by a standard metabolic profile typical of many woody dicotyledonous species. It utilizes the C3 photosynthetic pathway, a process in which carbon dioxide is fixed directly through the Calvin cycle without the specialized CO2-concentrating mechanisms found in C4 or CAM plants. Consequently, its carbon assimilation is highly dependent on stomatal conductance and environmental moisture levels. Furthermore, this species is not a nitrogen fixer, meaning it lacks the symbiotic relationship with nitrogen-fixing bacteria required to convert atmospheric nitrogen into usable forms; instead, it relies on the uptake of inorganic nitrogen, such as nitrates and ammonium, from the surrounding soil substrate to support its growth and protein synthesis.
