Glycine soja

What's the taxonomical classification of Glycine soja?

Glycine soja belongs to the kingdom Plantae and is classified within the phylum Streptophyta. As a member of the class Equisetopsida and the subclass Magnoliidae, it falls under the order Fabales. The plant is further categorized into the family Fabaceae and is specifically identified by its genus, Glycine, and its species, soja.

What are the morphological characteristics of this plant?

Glycine soja has an annual or perennial growth habit characterized by slender, climbing or trailing stems that can reach lengths of up to several meters. The leaves are trifoliate with ovate leaflets that possess entire margins and a pubescent surface. Small, papilionaceous flowers appear in axillary racemes, typically presenting in shades of white or pale purple. The plant develops pods that are covered in fine hairs and contain several small, spherical seeds. These seeds are often distinguished by a darker or more textured coat compared to their domesticated counterparts.

What parts of this plant are used medicinally?

This plant Glycine soja is utilized medicinally through its aerial parts, flowers, pods, roots, and seeds. The aerial parts are often harvested to prepare decoctions that address various inflammatory conditions. Medicinal preparations can also incorporate the flowers and pods to treat specific ailments related to heat and detoxification. The roots are frequently used in traditional formulations to support systemic health and balance. Additionally, the nutrient-dense seeds serve as a foundational component in many therapeutic applications.

According to a study published by "Planta medica", the aerial parts of Glycine soja were investigated to identify specific constituents. Researchers isolated and identified 4 known saponins, named soyasaponins I, II, III, and IV, which all share the same aglycone, soyasapogenol B. These saponins were examined for hepatoprotective actions against immunologically induced liver injury on primary cultured rat hepatocytes. The action of soyasaponin II was found to be almost comparable with soyasaponin I, while soyasaponins III and IV were more effective than soyasaponins I and II. Data indicates that the disaccharide group shows greater action than the trisaccharide group. Additionally, the saponin having a hexosyl unit shows a slightly greater action than the pentosyl unit within each disaccharide or trisaccharide group.

What medicinal compounds this plant contains?

This plant contains specific medicinal compounds such as eucomic acid and piscidic acid which contribute to its therapeutic potential. These organic acids are part of a complex chemical profile that offers various biological activities. Research into these constituents suggests they may play roles in supporting metabolic health and providing antioxidant effects. Beyond these specific acids, the plant also hosts a variety of other bioactive molecules like isoflavones and peptides. Studying these unique substances helps scientists understand how the plant can be utilized in pharmaceutical applications.

According to a study published by "Molecular plant", Glycine soja possesses black pods due to the presence of the L1 gene. This gene encodes a hydroxymethylglutaryl-coenzyme A (CoA) lyase-like (HMGL-like) domain protein that functions as a eucomic acid synthase. This enzyme facilitates the synthesis of two specific compounds, eucomic acid and piscidic acid. Both of these compounds contribute to the coloration of the pods and seed coats. The dark pigmentation resulting from these compounds increases photothermal efficiency.

This diet also led to an increase in the non-protein nitrogenous fraction of the skeletal musculature. Additionally, there was a decrease in the sarcoplasmic nitrogenous fraction of the muscle. These observations were documented during the 7-week study period.
