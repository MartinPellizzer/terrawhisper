Aspen, scientifically known as Populus tremula, is a deciduous tree valued in traditional medicine for its diverse therapeutic properties. Its bark contains salicylates, offering natural anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects. Often utilized to treat fevers and aches, this resilient species remains a significant subject in both ethnobotany and pharmacology.

The reproduction of Populus tremula is characterized by a dioecious sexual system, meaning male and female flowers are borne on separate individuals, and asexual reproduction is absent. The flowering period typically commences in March and concludes in April. The plant utilizes an abiotic pollination syndrome, specifically relying on wind (anemophily) for the transfer of pollen. Following successful pollination, the plant produces fruit in the form of a capsule, which is a dehiscence type. These capsules are relatively small, with a consistent mean fruit length of 0.5 cm (ranging from a minimum of 0.5 cm to a maximum of 0.5 cm). Self-fertilization is absent in this species. The seeds produced are extremely lightweight, with a mean mass of approximately 0.00010964575 g, a minimum mass of 7.41e-05 g, and a maximum mass of 0.000123 g. Dispersal is achieved through an anemochorous syndrome, where the wind acts as the primary agent for spreading the seeds.
