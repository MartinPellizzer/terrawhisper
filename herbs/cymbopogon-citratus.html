Cymbopogon citratus · Accepted scientific name

Scientific literature: Very Extensive (968 studies) · ★★★★★

Lemongrass, scientifically known as Cymbopogon citratus, is a tropical perennial grass renowned for its distinct citrus aroma and therapeutic properties. Widely used in traditional medicine, it possesses potent antibacterial, antifungal, and anti-inflammatory qualities. Often prepared as a soothing tea, it serves as a natural remedy for digestion and relaxation.

Life history

The life history of Cymbopogon citratus is characterized by its nature as a perennial plant, allowing it to persist through multiple growing seasons. It functions as a hemicryptophyte, a life form where the perennating buds are located at or just below the soil surface, providing protection during unfavorable environmental conditions. Furthermore, the plant exhibits an evergreen deciduousness, maintaining its foliage and photosynthetic capacity throughout much of its existence rather than shedding all leaves seasonally.

The morphology of Cymbopogon citratus is characterized by a graminoid growth form, functioning as a non-woody herb. It is a self-supporting plant that does not exhibit climbing or vine-like tendencies, nor does it function as a parasite, remaining an independent organism. As a terrestrial species, it is neither aquatic nor an epiphyte, growing directly in soil environments. The plant typically reaches a mean height of approximately 1.75 m, forming dense clumps of foliage characteristic of its botanical structure.

The physiology of Cymbopogon citratus is characterized by several distinct metabolic and structural adaptations that facilitate its growth in warm, tropical environments. Primarily, the plant utilizes a C4 photosynthetic pathway, which allows for more efficient carbon fixation and higher water-use efficiency compared to C3 plants, particularly under high light intensities and elevated temperatures. Structurally, the plant lacks succulence, meaning it does not possess specialized thickened tissues for significant water storage within its leaves or stems. Furthermore, Cymbopogon citratus is not a nitrogen fixer, as it lacks the symbiotic relationship with nitrogen-fixing bacteria required to convert atmospheric nitrogen into usable forms, thus relying instead on the availability of nitrogen within the soil substrate for its nutritional requirements.
