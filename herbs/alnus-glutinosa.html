Common alder, scientifically known as Alnus glutinosa, is a deciduous tree renowned for its ecological importance and medicinal versatility. Traditionally used in herbalism, its bark and leaves possess astringent, antiseptic, and anti-inflammatory properties. It has long been utilized to treat skin irritations, gastrointestinal issues, and various inflammatory conditions.

Ecology

The ecology of Alnus glutinosa is primarily defined by its preference for hydric environments, typically colonizing habitats such as lakes, rivers, shores, and swamps. As a riparian specialist, it thrives in waterlogged soils and marginal wetlands where it plays a crucial role in stabilizing banks and facilitating nitrogen fixation through symbiotic relationships with Frankia bacteria. This species exhibits a significant altitudinal tolerance, occupying various landscapes across a broad elevational range that reaches a maximum of 1500 m AMSL. Its ability to endure periodically anaerobic soil conditions allows it to dominate transitional zones between aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems, forming dense thickets that provide essential structural complexity to wetland communities.

Reproduction

The reproduction of Alnus glutinosa is characterized by a dioecious sexual reproduction system, where asexual reproduction is absent and self-fertilization is also absent. The flowering process typically occurs in April, with the flowering period spanning from the start to the end of the month. Pollination is an abiotic process mediated by the wind (wind pollination syndrome). The plant produces dry, indehiscent fruits categorized as achenes, which have an average length of 0.75 cm. The resulting seeds are quite small, featuring a mean seed length of 2.7 mm (ranging from a minimum to maximum of 2.7 mm) and a consistent seed volume of 1.8 mm³. The seed mass is highly variable, with a mean mass of approximately 0.002033712 g, ranging from a minimum of 0.0009 g to a maximum of 0.0059 g. For the spread of its progeny, the plant utilizes an anemochorous dispersal syndrome, relying on the wind to transport its seeds.
