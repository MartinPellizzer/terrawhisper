Cassytha filiformis

What's the taxonomical classification of Cassytha filiformis?

Cassytha filiformis belongs to the kingdom Plantae and is classified within the phylum Streptophyta. Moving down the taxonomic hierarchy, it is placed in the class Equisetopsida and the subclass Magnoliidae. It falls under the order Laurales and is a member of the family Lauraceae. Within this family, the plant is categorized under the genus Cassytha, and its specific identification is determined by its species name, filiformis.

What are the morphological characteristics of this plant?

Cassytha filiformis has narrow, linear leaves that are typically small, densely arranged along slender, wiry branches, and it produces small, inconspicuous flowers. The plant characteristically exhibits a spreading or scrambling growth habit, often forming dense, tangled thickets. Its stems are woody and can become quite flexible, allowing the shrub to weave through surrounding vegetation. The foliage is often described as having a fine, thread-like appearance due to the extremely thin leaf shape. This morphological structure helps the species adapt to its specific environmental niches within the landscape.

What is the geographical distribution of this plant?

This plant is native to the tropical and subtropical regions of eastern Australia, specifically stretching from northern Queensland down through parts of New South Wales. It is most commonly found inhabiting coastal heathlands and littoral rainforests where sandy soils are prevalent. The species thrives in diverse habitats ranging from rocky outcrops to more sheltered forest edges along the coastline. Its distribution is closely tied to maritime climates that provide sufficient humidity and warmth. This limited range makes it a characteristic element of the specific coastal ecosystems found in these southeastern Australian territories.

How is this plant cultivated?

This plant requires well-draining, sandy or rocky soil and full sunlight to thrive in warm, tropical to subtropical climates.

You should water it moderately, allowing the substrate to dry out between applications to prevent root rot. Because it is a climbing or scrambling shrub, providing a trellis or support structure will encourage its natural growth habit. Regular pruning helps maintain its shape and promotes denser foliage throughout the growing season.

Avoid over-fertilizing, as excessive nitrogen can lead to weak stem development.

What parts of this plant are used medicinally?

This plant Cassytha filiformis is utilized medicinally through its bark, the herb, and the whole plant. The bark is frequently processed to extract compounds that address various ailments in traditional healing practices. Additionally, the herb component is often prepared as a decoction or infusion to treat specific physiological conditions. Practitioners may also employ the whole plant to ensure a full spectrum of bioactive constituents is captured during treatment. These different parts are essential to the traditional pharmacological applications of the species.

According to a study published by "TheScientificWorldJournal", the bark of Cassytha filiformis is used in Beninese traditional medicine to treat urinary tract infections. Plant materials were extracted using 70% ethanol to produce a hydroethanolic extract. This extract showed antibacterial activity against Staphylococci and uropathogenic Enterobacteria with inhibition zones of 13.8-21.2 mm and MICs of 3.5-9 mg/mL. At a dose of 200 mg/kg, the hydroethanolic extracts significantly reduced acetic acid-induced pain. Additionally, the plant exhibited diuretic activity comparable to furosemide. No toxic effects were observed at a dosage of 2000 mg/kg over 14 days.

What medicinal compounds this plant contains?

This plant contains the medicinal compounds cassyformine, cassythic acid, cassythine, cathafiline, and cathaformine. These specialized alkaloids and organic acids are primarily concentrated within the roots of the species. Researchers study these specific chemical constituents to understand their potential pharmacological activities in human health. Each unique molecule contributes to the overall bioactive profile of the plant material. Detailed chemical analysis helps identify how these substances interact with biological systems.

According to a study published by "Journal of natural products", three new compounds were isolated and characterized from the MeOH extract of the fresh herbs of Cassytha filiformis using a bioassay-directed fractionation method. These new substances include the aporpine alkaloid cassyformine (4), the oxoaaporphine alkaloid filiformine (8), and the lignan (+)-diasyringaresinol (10). In addition to these three new findings, 14 known compounds were identified from the plant. Among the specific isolates, cathafiline (1), cathaformine (2), actinodaphnine (3), N-methylactinodaphnine (5), predicentrine (6), and ocoteine (7) exhibited significant antiplatelet aggregation activity.

What side effects this plant can have?

This plant can cause significant physiological changes including alterations in aspartate aminotransferase levels, diuretic activity, hypercholesterolemic effects, pain, and a reduced absolute weight of the kidneys. These biochemical shifts suggest that the species may impact liver enzyme functions and lipid metabolism. The observed diuretic activity indicates an effect on renal fluid regulation, which correlates with the physical reduction in kidney weight. Furthermore, subjects may experience physical discomfort or pain as a secondary response to these systemic changes. Such diverse side effects highlight the complex pharmacological profile of the plant.

According to a study published by "Journal of toxicology", the subacute toxicity of the hydroethanolic extract of the Cassytha filiformis whole plant was evaluated in male Wistar albino rats at three different doses of 200, 400, and 800 mg/kg. While no mortality or structural alterations of the liver and kidneys were found, significant alterations in certain hematological and biochemical parameters including hematocrit, ASAT, and uremia were noted in the lot of rats treated with Cassytha filiformis extracts. Regarding larval cytotoxicity, the extract was found not to be cytotoxic with an LC50 > 0.1 mg/mL. These findings suggest that the use of this plant in traditional medicine at high doses and repeated over a long period of time requires special attention.
