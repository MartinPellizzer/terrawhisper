Moringa, scientifically known as Moringa oleifera, is a nutrient-dense powerhouse often referred to as the "miracle tree." Native to parts of India, this versatile plant offers exceptional health benefits. Its leaves, seeds, and pods are packed with essential vitamins, minerals, and antioxidants, making it a vital medicinal resource.

Distribution

This plant, Moringa oleifera, exhibits a diverse geographical distribution across several warm regions. In Northern Africa, it can be found growing in the country of Libya. Its presence extends to the Macaronesian islands, specifically within Cape Verde. In West Tropical Africa, the species is established in Benin and Burkina. Additionally, the plant is widely distributed throughout the Gambia.

Life history

The life history of Moringa oleifera is characterized by its nature as a perennial plant, allowing it to persist through multiple growing seasons. As a phanerophyte, its reproductive shoots are elevated well above the ground, typically manifesting as a small to medium-sized tree. The plant exhibits a deciduous growth habit, shedding its leaves periodically, often in response to seasonal changes or periods of drought. This combination of a long-lived perennial lifecycle and a phanerophyte life form ensures its ability to establish a dominant presence in its native tropical and subtropical habitats.

Reproduction

The reproduction of Moringa oleifera is characterized by a biotic pollination syndrome, primarily facilitated by bees. The flowering process involves inflorescences that vary in length, ranging from a minimum of 0.1 m to a maximum of 0.3 m. Following successful pollination, the plant develops a capsule-type fruit. These fruits exhibit significant morphological variation, with lengths ranging from a minimum of 20 cm to a maximum of 50 cm, maintaining an average length of 35 cm. The fruit is dehiscent, meaning it splits open at maturity to facilitate seed release. The resulting seeds show specific mass characteristics, with a minimum mass of 0.20202 g, a maximum of 0.25 g, and a mean seed mass of approximately 0.219675 g. Once released, the seeds utilize an anemochorous dispersal syndrome, relying on wind for movement and colonization.
