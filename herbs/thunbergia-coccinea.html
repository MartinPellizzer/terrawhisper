Thunbergia coccinea

What's the taxonomical classification of Thunbergia coccinea?

Thunbergia coccinea belongs to the kingdom Plantae and is classified under the phylum Streptophyta. Within the class Equisetopsida and the subclass Magnoliidae, it is organized under the order Lamiales. This plant is a member of the family Acanthaceae, specifically falling within the genus Thunbergia, with its unique scientific designation being the species coccinea.

What are the morphological characteristics of this plant?

Thunbergia coccinea has a twining, perennial woody vine morphology characterized by slender, creeping stems and bright red, trumpet-shaped flowers. The foliage consists of dark green, ovate to cordate leaves with slightly serrated margins and a somewhat textured surface. Each flower emerges from a tubular calyx, displaying a vibrant scarlet hue that attracts pollinators. The plant typically develops a dense, scrambling habit as its stems coil around supporting structures. These climbing stems allow the species to spread rapidly across garden trellises or through surrounding vegetation.

What is the geographical distribution of this plant?

This plant is native to the arid, rocky regions of South Africa, specifically within the provinces of the Northern Cape and Western Cape. It primarily thrives in the succulent Karoo and Fynbos biomes where environmental conditions are harsh and dry. Due to its ability to tolerate drought and poor soil, it has been widely introduced to other subtropical climates around the world. Many gardeners in Mediterranean-style environments cultivate it for its vibrant red flowers and resilience. In these non-native locations, it is often used as a decorative groundcover or accent plant.

According to a study published by "Cellular and molecular biology (Noisy-le-Grand, France)", the extracted essential oils of Thunbergia coccinea were tested for their antibacterial, bactericidal, and antioxidant properties. The research utilized methods such as disc diffusion and the deduction of minimum inhibitory concentration through microbroth dilution assays. Additionally, the antioxidant activity was evaluated using the DPPH free radical scavenging assay, MDA, Hydrogen peroxide radical inhibition assay, and Superoxide radical inhibition assay (O 2 -). The extract of the plant at varying concentrations of 20, 40, 60, 80 and 100 mg/mL demonstrated noteworthy (P< 0.001) anthelmintic action. This anthelmintic effect showed an effective change when the dose was adjusted.
