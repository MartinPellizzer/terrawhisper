Bigleaf Lupin, scientifically known as Lupinus polyphyllus, is a striking perennial herb native to the Pacific Northwest. Renowned for its dramatic, towering spikes of violet flowers, this plant is valued in traditional medicine for its nitrogen-fixing capabilities and potential therapeutic properties, though it requires careful handling due to alkaloid content.

Reproduction

The reproduction of Lupinus polyphyllus is driven by sexual processes, as asexual reproduction is notably absent. The flowering period typically commences in June and extends through August. This reproductive cycle relies on biotic pollination, specifically utilizing an insect pollination syndrome with a strong specialization toward bees. The plant is characterized by the absence of self-fertilization, necessitating cross-pollination. Following fertilization, the plant produces fruit in the form of a capsule, which reaches an average length of 4 cm. These fruits are dehiscent, meaning they split open at maturity to facilitate seed dispersal. The dispersal syndrome is classified as autochorous, where the plant utilizes its own mechanisms to scatter seeds. The resulting seeds are relatively uniform in size, with a mean length of 2 mm (ranging from a minimum to a maximum of 2 mm) and a mean mass of approximately 0.022145875 g, though individual seed mass can vary between 0.014725 g and 0.0283 g.
