Physiology

The physiology of Cereus fernambuccensis is characterized by specialized metabolic adaptations typical of succulent cacti, primarily focused on water conservation and efficient photosynthetic pathways. As a CAM (Crassulacean Acid Metabolism) plant, it exhibits temporal separation of carbon fixation, opening its stomata during the cooler nocturnal hours to absorb carbon dioxide, which is then stored as malate to minimize transpirational water loss during the heat of the day. This metabolic strategy allows the plant to thrive in arid environments where water scarcity is a limiting factor. Regarding its nutritional acquisition and soil interaction, the plant does not function as a nitrogen fixer; it lacks the symbiotic relationship with diazotrophic bacteria, such as Rhizobium or Frankia, necessary to convert atmospheric nitrogen into bioavailable forms. Instead, it relies on the uptake of nitrates and ammonium from the soil through its root system, often supplemented by the organic decomposition within its immediate microhabitat. The plant's internal structure is dominated by water-storing parenchyma tissue, which maintains turgor pressure and provides a physiological buffer against prolonged drought periods.
