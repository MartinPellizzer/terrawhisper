Ecology

The ecology of Ficus pertusa is characterized by a highly versatile distribution across diverse Brazilian biomes, including the Caatinga (strictu sensu), Cerrado (lato sensu), Floresta Ciliar or Galeria, Floresta Estacional Decidual, Floresta Estacional Semi-decidual, Floresta Ombrófila (Pluvial), and Restinga. This broad adaptability allows the species to thrive in varied environmental conditions, ranging from semi-arid scrublands to lush, humid rainforests and coastal restingas. In terms of topography, the species exhibits a wide altitudinal tolerance, spanning from sea level (0 m AMSL) up to a maximum elevation of 2000 m AMSL. Despite this expansive range, its distribution is centered around a mean elevation of approximately 295 m AMSL, indicating a preference for lowland to mid-elevation habitats within its various ecological niches.

Life history

The life history of Ficus pertusa is characterized by its status as a perennial organism, allowing it to persist through multiple growing seasons and maintain a stable presence within its ecological niche. As a phanerophyte, its regenerative buds are located high above the ground on woody stems, a structural strategy that facilitates its development into a substantial woody form. This phanerophyte life form ensures that the plant can withstand various environmental pressures by protecting its vital meristematic tissues, contributing to its long-term survival and continuous life cycle.

The morphology of Ficus pertusa is characterized by a woody growth form, primarily manifesting as a tree. While it functions as an independent organism rather than a parasite, it exhibits a hemiepiphyte life strategy, often beginning its life cycle in an epiphytic stage before establishing itself as a terrestrial plant. As a terrestrial species, it does not possess aquatic or semiaquatic characteristics and maintains a robust, self-supporting structure without relying on climbing habits like lianas or vines.

Physiology

The physiology of Ficus pertusa is characterized by its specific metabolic and nutritional processes, most notably its reliance on the C3 photosynthetic pathway. As a C3 plant, it utilizes the Calvin cycle to fix atmospheric carbon dioxide, a process where the first stable product is a three-carbon molecule, typically occurring under standard environmental conditions without the specialized carbon-concentrating mechanisms found in C4 or CAM species. Furthermore, regarding its nutrient acquisition strategies, the plant is not a nitrogen fixer; it lacks the biological mechanisms, such as symbiotic relationships with nitrogen-fixing bacteria, required to convert atmospheric nitrogen into usable forms, meaning it must rely on the uptake of nitrogenous compounds directly from the soil through its root system.
