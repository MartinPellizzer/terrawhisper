Stephanotis volubilis

Stephanotis volubilis, commonly known as Madagascar jasmine, is a stunning climbing vine prized for its fragrant, waxy white blooms. Beyond its ornamental beauty, this evergreen plant holds traditional medicinal significance, often utilized in various cultures for its potential therapeutic properties and aromatic essences used in holistic wellness practices.

  • Scientific name: Stephanotis volubilis
  • Family: Apocynaceae
  • Native range: Asia-Temperate

Taxonomical Classification

This plant belongs to the kingdom Plantae within the phylum Streptophyta and is classified under the class Equisetopsida. As a member of the subclass Magnoliidae and the order Gentianales, it falls under the family Apocynaceae. Finally, its taxonomic identity is defined by the genus Stephanotis.

Common Names

This plant, Stephanotis volubilis, is known by various names depending on the linguistic context. In English, it is most commonly referred to as Madagascar jasmine, while in French, it is called jasmin de Madagascar. In German, the plant is known as Madagaskar-Jasmin, and in Spanish, it is called jazmín de Madagascar.

Distribution

This plant exhibits a broad geographical distribution across several key regions in Asia. In China, it can be found throughout the South-Central and Southeast territories. Its presence extends to Eastern Asia, specifically within the island of Taiwan. Moving toward the Indian Subcontinent, the species is documented in the state of Assam. Additionally, the plant is also native to the regions of Bangladesh.

Plant Parts

This plant, Stephanotis verticillata (often referred to in historical contexts as Stephanotis volubilis), is a climbing evergreen shrub characterized by its distinctive structural components. The twigs are slender and flexible, allowing the plant to scramble and twine effectively around supporting structures to reach sunlight. Complementing these climbing stems are the lush, dark green leaves, which are typically thick, leathery, and elliptical in shape, providing the necessary surface area for photosynthesis to fuel its fragrant flowering process.
