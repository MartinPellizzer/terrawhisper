Cinnamomum glanduliferum belongs to the kingdom Plantae and is classified under the phylum Streptophyta. Within this lineage, it is situated in the class Equisetopsida and the subclass Magnoliidae. The taxonomic hierarchy continues through the order Laurales and the family Lauraceae, leading to its specific classification within the genus Cinnamomum. Ultimately, the plant is identified by its unique species name, glanduliferum.

What are the morphological characteristics of this plant?

Cinnamomum glanduliferum has elliptical to lanceolate leaves that are characterized by prominent glandular dots on the underside and an entire or slightly undulate margin. The foliage typically exhibits a glossy, dark green upper surface and a paler, more textured ventral side. These leaves are arranged in an opposite pattern along the woody stems of the shrub or small tree. Small, inconspicuous flowers emerge from the leaf axils, eventually developing into small fleshy fruits. The overall architecture of the plant is compact, reflecting its growth habit in understory environments.

What are the pharmacological activities of Cinnamomum glanduliferum?

This plant has potent antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory pharmacological activities. Research indicates that its essential oils and extracts effectively inhibit the growth of various pathogenic bacteria and fungi. These bioactive compounds work by disrupting microbial cell membranes or interfering with essential metabolic processes. Additionally, the plant demonstrates a significant capacity to neutralize free radicals within biological systems. Such diverse properties suggest that Cinnamonum glanduliferum holds considerable potential for developing new therapeutic agents.

According to a study published by "Pharmaceuticaal biology", the essential oils of Cinnamomum glanduliferum exhibit significant antimicrobial potential against various foodborne pathogenic and spoilage bacteria. Using a serial double dilution method, the oil was tested at final concentrations ranging from 0.85 to 440.0 µg/ml in a 96 microtiter plate. The in vitro results showed that the oil was very active against Gram-negative bacteria, specifically A. salmonicida with an MIC of 1.72 µg/ml. Additionally, the oil demonstrated activity against E. coli and P. aeruginosa, both of which had an MIC of 3.43 µg/ml. These findings suggest that the oil may be used in the treatment of diseases caused by these specific bacteria.
