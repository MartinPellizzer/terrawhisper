Black-cumin

Nigella sativa · Accepted scientific name

Scientific literature: Very Extensive (1887 studies) · ★★★★★

Black-cumin, scientifically known as Nigella sativa, is a versatile medicinal plant celebrated for its profound therapeutic properties. Often referred to as "black seed," this nutrient-dense spice contains thymoquinone, a powerful compound known for its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial effects, making it a cornerstone in traditional and modern wellness.

The life history of Nigella sativa is characterized by an annual lifecycle, meaning the plant completes its entire developmental process—from germination to seed production—within a single growing season. Physiology

The physiology of Nigella sativa is characterized by a C3 photosynthetic pathway, meaning it utilizes the Calvin cycle to fix carbon dioxide through the enzyme Rubisco, which is typical for many herbaceous annuals in the Ranunculaceae family. This metabolic process involves the direct fixation of CO2 into three-carbon compounds, making the plant's carbon assimilation efficiency highly dependent on environmental conditions such as temperature and water availability. Furthermore, Nigella sativa is not a nitrogen fixer; it lacks the specialized symbiotic relationship with rhizobia or other nitrogen-fixing bacteria required to convert atmospheric nitrogen into a usable form, and therefore relies on the uptake of nitrates and ammonium from the soil to satisfy its nutritional requirements for growth and development.

Reproduction

The reproductive cycle of Nigella sativa is characterized by a distinct seasonal flowering period that typically begins in June and concludes in August. The plant utilizes biotic pollination syndromes, specifically relying on insects such as bees to facilitate the fertilization process. Once fertilization occurs, the plant develops fruit in the form of a capsule, with a consistent length measuring approximately 1 cm (ranging from a minimum to a maximum of 1 cm). These capsules are dehiscent, meaning they split open to release the seeds. The resulting seeds are quite small, possessing a mean mass of 0.00141 g, with individual seed weights ranging from a minimum of 0.00051 g to a maximum of 0.00231 g. Regarding seed dispersal, the plant employs an unspecialized syndrome alongside anthropochorous dispersal, indicating that while it lacks a highly specialized natural mechanism, human activity plays a significant role in its distribution.
