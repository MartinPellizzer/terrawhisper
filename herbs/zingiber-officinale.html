Scientific literature: Very Extensive (2804 studies) · ★★★★★

Ginger, scientifically known as Zingiber officinale, is a versatile rhizomatous herb celebrated for its potent medicinal properties. Renowned for its warming aroma and spicy flavor, it contains bioactive compounds like gingerol. This powerful plant is widely used to alleviate nausea, reduce inflammation, and support digestive health and immunity.

Finally, it is identified by the genus Zingiber, specifically known as Zingiber officinale.

Distribution

This plant exhibits a widespread presence across several tropical regions. In West-Central Tropical Africa, its distribution includes the Gulf of Guinea Islands. The species is also well-established throughout the Western Indian Ocean. Specifically, it can be found in the Comoros, Mauritius, and Madagascar. Finally, its geographical range extends to the island of Réunion within the same oceanic region.

Life history

The life history of Zingiber officinale is characterized by its status as a perennial plant, allowing it to persist through multiple growing seasons. As a member of the ginger family, its biological structure is defined by its classification as a geophyte, meaning it survives unfavorable environmental conditions by storing nutrients in underground rhizomes. Furthermore, it is categorized as a cryptophyte, as its vital regenerative organs are concealed beneath the soil surface, ensuring its survival and ability to re-emerge during favorable periods.

Physiology

The physiology of Zingiber officinale is characterized by a C3 photosynthetic pathway, which dictates its metabolic processes and carbon fixation mechanisms within the leaf tissues. Unlike plants utilizing C4 or CAM pathways, its carbon assimilation is highly dependent on the efficiency of the enzyme RuBisCO under varying light and temperature conditions. In terms of water management and morphology, the plant does not exhibit succulence, lacking the specialized thickened tissues typically used for significant water storage in arid-adapted species. Furthermore, Zingiber officinale is not a nitrogen fixer; it lacks the symbiotic relationship with nitrogen-fixing bacteria required to convert atmospheric nitrogen into usable forms, meaning it relies on the uptake of inorganic nitrogen from the soil to support its physiological development and biomass production.

Reproduction

Reproduction in Zingiber officinale occurs through both sexual and asexual mechanisms, though its propagation in both natural and cultivated contexts is characterized by a diverse range of dispersal syndromes. While the plant can produce seeds through the pollination of its flowering inflorescences, its primary mode of multiplication is vegetative, utilizing underground rhizomes that spread laterally to form new clonal colonies. This vegetative expansion is categorized under various dispersal modes, including autochorous movement through rhizomatous growth, while its broader ecological and human-mediated movement encompasses anemochorous, anthropochorous, endozoochorous, epizoochorous, hydrochorous, myrmecocorous, unspecialized, and zoochorous syndromes. Specifically, the plant is notably anthropochorous due to extensive human intervention in its cultivation and global distribution, supplemented by the potential for natural dispersal via wind, water, animals, and insects.
