The ecology of Sequoia sempervirens is characterized by a preference for lowland habitats, where it thrives in environments that provide the necessary moisture and fog drip essential for its massive growth. This species relies on specific climatic conditions, particularly the heavy maritime fog that mitigates summer droughts and provides a consistent water source to its canopy.

The life history of Sequoia sempervirens is characterized by a long-lived perennial lifecycle, allowing it to dominate forest ecosystems for centuries. As a phanerophyte, its regenerative buds are located high above the ground on woody branches, a structural adaptation that facilitates its massive vertical growth. Throughout its existence, the plant maintains an evergreen deciduousness pattern, ensuring that its photosynthetic tissues remain functional year-round to support its extensive metabolic needs. This combination of a permanent, long-term lifecycle and a robust phanerophyte life form enables the species to maintain a consistent presence in its habitat across vast temporal scales.

Reproduction

The reproduction of Sequoia sempervirens is characterized by a primarily sexual process, as asexual reproduction is absent. The species is monoecious, bearing both male and female reproductive structures on the same individual. Pollination is driven by an abiotic syndrome, specifically utilizing wind to facilitate the transfer of pollen. The reproductive output is contained within fruits that possess an average length and width of 2.2 cm. Within these fruits, the seeds exhibit a consistent length of 5.5 mm (with both minimum and maximum values recorded at this measurement). Following maturation, the plant employs an anemochorous dispersal syndrome, relying on wind to transport its seeds away from the parent tree.
