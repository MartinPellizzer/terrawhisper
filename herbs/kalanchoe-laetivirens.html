As a succulent plant, it possesses thickened, fleshy leaves and stems that function as primary reservoirs for water storage, allowing the organism to maintain hydration during extended periods of drought. This morphological trait is inextricably linked to its Crassulacean Acid Metabolism (CAM), a physiological pathway wherein the plant opens its stomata at night to fix carbon dioxide, thereby minimizing transpirational water loss that would occur under direct sunlight. During the nocturnal phase, CO2 is converted into organic acids stored within the vacuoles of the succulent tissues; during the diurnal phase, these acids are decarboxylated to provide the carbon necessary for photosynthesis while the stomata remain closed. This highly efficient water-use strategy, combined with its ability to manage osmotic pressure within its specialized parenchyma cells, enables the plant to thrive in arid or semi-arid environments where water availability is inconsistent.
