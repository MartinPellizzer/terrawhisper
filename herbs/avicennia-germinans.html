Avicennia germinans

Avicennia germinans, commonly known as the black mangrove, is a resilient halophyte essential to coastal ecosystems. Beyond its ecological role in stabilizing shorelines, this plant holds significant medicinal potential. Traditional practices utilize its various parts to treat inflammation and skin ailments, offering promising avenues for modern pharmacological research.

  • Scientific name: Avicennia germinans
  • Family: Acanthaceae
  • Native range: Africa

Taxonomical Classification

This plant belongs to the kingdom Plantae within the phylum Streptophyta and the class Equisetopsida. It is classified under the subclass Magnoliidae in the order Lamiales, falling within the family Acanthaceae. Finally, its taxonomic placement is completed by its designation under the genus Avicennia.

Distribution

This plant, Avicennia germinans, exhibits a specific geographical distribution throughout the coastal regions of West Tropical Africa. Its presence is documented in the nation of Benin, where it thrives in suitable habitats. Furthermore, it can be found stretching across the western reaches of the continent in Gambia. The species also inhabits the coastal areas of Guinea-Bissau and Ghana. Finally, its range extends into the tropical landscapes of Guinea, marking its presence across these diverse West African territories.

Plant Parts

This plant, Avicennia germinans, commonly known as the black mangrove, utilizes its specialized leaves to thrive in harsh, saline environments. These leaves are typically thick and leathery, featuring a distinctively smooth, dark green upper surface and a paler underside. To manage high salt concentrations, the leaves possess specialized salt glands that actively excrete excess salt, often leaving visible white crystals on their surface. Beyond salt regulation, the leaves are essential for photosynthesis and serve as a primary source of medicinal compounds used in various traditional applications.
