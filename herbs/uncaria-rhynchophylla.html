Uncaria rhynchophylla

Uncaria rhynchophylla ex

Uncaria rhynchophylla, commonly known as Cat's Claw, is a woody vine native to the Amazon rainforest. Renowned in traditional medicine, it contains bioactive alkaloids with potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. Today, it is widely studied for its ability to support immune function and alleviate various inflammatory conditions naturally.

  • Scientific name: Uncaria rhynchophylla
  • Family: Rubiaceae
  • Native range: Asia-Temperate

Taxonomical Classification

This plant belongs to the kingdom Plantae and is classified within the phylum Streptophyta and class Equisetopsida. It is further categorized under the subclass Magnoliidae and the order Gentianales, falling within the family Rubiaceae. Finally, its specific taxonomic identity is defined by the genus Uncaria.

Distribution

This plant exhibits a broad geographical distribution across several key regions of East and Southeast Asia. In China, it can be found specifically throughout the South-Central and Southeast territories. Its presence extends further into Eastern Asia, where it is documented in both Japan and Taiwan. Additionally, the species is located within the Indo-China region, specifically in Vietnam. Together, these locations define the natural habitat range for Uncaria rhynchophylla.

Plant Parts

This plant, known scientifically as Uncaria rhynchophylla, exhibits specialized morphological features characteristic of its climbing nature. As a woody vine, it utilizes sharp, curved hooks to anchor itself and ascend toward the sunlight. The foliage consists of a lush green leaf structure that facilitates photosynthesis, while the long, climbing stems provide the necessary framework for growth. The overall architecture of the plant is supported by sturdy, elongated stems that weave through the forest canopy. Most importantly, for medicinal purposes, the stem bark is the primary part harvested, as it contains the concentrated bioactive alkaloids used in traditional and modern pharmacology.
