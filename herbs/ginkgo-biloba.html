Ginkgo biloba · Accepted scientific name

Scientific literature: Very Extensive (2603 studies) · ★★★★★

Maidenhair tree, scientifically known as Ginkgo biloba, is a living fossil with a history spanning millions of years. Renowned for its unique fan-shaped leaves, this resilient species is widely used in traditional medicine to support cognitive function, enhance memory, and improve circulation, offering profound benefits for brain health.

Life history

The life history of Ginkgo biloba is characterized by its status as a perennial organism, meaning it persists through multiple growing seasons and can live for centuries, often reaching extreme longevity. As a phanerophyte, its reproductive buds are borne on branches well above the ground, a growth habit that allows it to develop into a large, woody tree. This phanerophyte life form ensures that its vital structures are elevated, protected from many ground-level disturbances, contributing to its robust and enduring presence in its natural ecosystem.

Physiology

The physiology of Ginkgo biloba is characterized by a complex suite of metabolic and structural adaptations, most notably its utilization of the C3 photosynthetic pathway. In this process, carbon dioxide is fixed directly into a three-carbon compound through the enzyme RuBisCO during the Calvin cycle, a mechanism typical of most woody gymnosperms. The plant's metabolic profile is further distinguished by the synthesis of specialized secondary metabolites, specifically terpenoids (such as ginkgolides and bilobalide) and flavonoid glycosides, which serve crucial physiological roles in protecting the cellular machinery from oxidative stress. These compounds act as potent antioxidants, mitigating damage caused by reactive oxygen species (ROS) produced during photorespiration or environmental stressors. Additionally, Ginkgo biloba exhibits unique vascular physiology; its tracheids facilitate water and mineral transport, while its specialized leaf anatomy, featuring a distinct cuticle and stomatal regulation, assists in managing transpiration rates and maintaining osmotic balance. The plant's longevity and resilience are fundamentally linked to these integrated physiological processes, which allow it to maintain cellular homeostasis across diverse environmental conditions.
