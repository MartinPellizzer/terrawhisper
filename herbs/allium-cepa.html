Onion, scientifically known as Allium cepa, is a versatile bulbous plant celebrated for both culinary and medicinal properties. Rich in antioxidants and sulfur compounds, it offers significant health benefits, including anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial effects. Historically utilized in traditional medicine, it remains a powerful tool for supporting overall wellness.

The physiology of Allium cepa is characterized by a C3 photosynthetic pathway, utilizing the Calvin cycle to fix carbon dioxide during the light-independent reactions. The plant does not exhibit succulence, lacking the specialized water-storage tissues found in succulent species, and it is classified as non-carnivorous, relying solely on soil-based nutrient uptake rather than capturing insects. Furthermore, Allium cepa is not a nitrogen fixer, meaning it lacks the symbiotic relationship with nitrogen-fixing bacteria required to convert atmospheric nitrogen into usable forms, and instead depends on the availability of nitrates and ammonium within the substrate.

Reproduction

The reproduction of Allium cepa is characterized by a sexual process, as asexual reproduction is noted to be absent. The flowering period typically commences in July and concludes in August, featuring flowers that range in length from a minimum of 0.5 cm to a maximum of 0.7 cm and are green in color. The pollination syndrome is primarily biotic, specifically relying on insects for the transfer of pollen. Following successful pollination, the plant produces dry fruit in the form of a capsule. This fruit is dehiscent, meaning it opens at maturity to facilitate seed release. The dispersal syndrome is classified as autochorous, where seeds are dispersed by the plant itself. The resulting seeds are quite small, with a mean seed mass of 0.004161 g (ranging from a minimum of 0.00223 g to a maximum of 0.00372 g) and a consistent seed volume of 3.1 mm³.
