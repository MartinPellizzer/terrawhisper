Nandina domestica

What's the taxonomical classification of Nandina domestica?

Nandina domestica belongs to the kingdom Plantae and is classified under the phylum Streptophyta. Within this group, it is categorized under the class Equisetopsida and the subclass Magnoliidae. Its evolutionary lineage continues through the order Ranunculales and the family Berberidaceae. Finally, the plant is defined by the genus Nandina and is specifically identified as the species domesticus.

What are the morphological characteristics of this plant?

Nandina domestica has compound, pinnate leaves that consist of multiple small, lanceolate leaflets arranged symmetrically along a central stem. These leaves often exhibit striking color shifts from bright green to shades of red or bronze depending on the season and temperature. The plant produces upright, woody stems that can reach several feet in height. Small, clusters of white flowers emerge in the spring from the terminal ends of the branches. Following the bloom, the plant develops bright red berries that persist on the foliage throughout much of the winter.

How is this plant cultivated?

This plant Nandina domestica grows best in well-drained soil within USDA hardiness zones 7 through 10 and requires full sun to partial shade for optimal foliage color.

It thrives when planted in slightly acidic to neutral soil that maintains consistent moisture without becoming waterlogged. Regular mulching around the base helps retain soil moisture and suppress competing weeds during the growing season. While the species is remarkably drought-tolerant once established, providing supplemental water during extreme heat promotes healthier growth.

Pruning should be done in late winter or early spring to maintain a compact shape and encourage new shoots.

According to a study published by "Bioscience, biotechnology, and biochemistry", the tyrosinase inhibitory activity of extracts from 53 parts of 36 plant species cultivated in Okinawa was investigated. This compound, 4-beta-D-glucopyranosyloxybenzoic acid, had its inhibitory activity toward tyrosinase revealed for the first time in this work.

What side effects this plant can have?

This plant can cause nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, skin irritation, and dizziness if ingested or touched. The berries and stems contain cyanogenic glycosides that trigger severe gastrointestinal distress such as abdominal pain and vomiting upon consumption. Direct contact with the foliage or berries may also lead to localized skin irritation for sensitive individuals. Systemic toxicity can further manifest as dizziness, signaling a more serious reaction to the plant's toxins. Therefore, caution is necessary when handling this species to avoid these various adverse health effects.
