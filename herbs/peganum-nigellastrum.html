Peganum nigellastrum

What's the taxonomical classification of Peganum nigellastrum?

Peganum nigellastrum belongs to the kingdom Plantae and is classified under the phylum Streptophyta. Within this group, it is categorized under the class Equisetopsida and the subclass Magnoliidae. Moving further down the taxonomic hierarchy, it falls under the order Sapindales and is a member of the family Nitrariaceae. Finally, the plant is identified by its genus, Peganum, and its specific species designation, nigellastrum.

What are the morphological characteristics of this plant?

Peganum nigellasatrum has an erect, herbaceous growth habit characterized by deeply divided, pinnatisect leaves and solitary, actinomorphic flowers. The foliage consists of narrow, linear segments that are arranged alternately along the stem. Its floral structure features five white petals surrounding a central cluster of numerous stamens and a prominent gynoecium. As the plant matures, it produces small, globose capsules that house numerous tiny, dark seeds. These botanical features collectively define its distinctive visual profile within its natural habitat.

How is this plant cultivated?

This plant Peganum nigellaspermum requires well-draining soil and full sun exposure to produce its characteristic black seeds effectively.

Growers should sow the seeds directly into the ground in early spring or late autumn as the species does not tolerate transplanting well. Maintaining consistent moisture during the germination phase is crucial, though the soil must not remain waterlogged to prevent root rot. Once established, the plant is relatively drought-tolerant and thrives in temperate climates with moderate rainfall.

Regular weeding around the base helps ensure the plant receives sufficient nutrients for vigorous growth.

What parts of this plant are used medicinally?

This plant Peganum nigellasrum is utilized medicinally through the application of its aerial parts and its roots. The aerial parts, which include the leaves and seeds, are frequently processed to extract essential oils and bioactive compounds. These components are often used in traditional remedies to address various inflammatory or digestive issues. Additionally, the roots contain specific phytochemicals that contribute to the plant's overall therapeutic profile. Researchers study these different sections to better understand their individual pharmacological properties.

According to a study published by "Phytochemistry", the aerial parts of Peganum nigellastrum are used to isolate medicinal compounds. From these parts, 2 canthin-6-one alkaloids known as luotonins C and D were isolated. Additionally, 2 phenylpropanoids, dihydrosinapyl ferulate and dihydroconiferyl ferulate, were identified. The study also isolated 4 known alkaloids, which are harmine, 3-phenylquinoline, 3-(4-hydroxyphenyl)quinoline and 3-(1H-indol-3-yl)quinoline.

What medicinal compounds this plant contains?

This plant contains the medicinal compounds 3-(1H-indol-3-yl)quinoline, 3-(4-hydroxyphenyl)quinoline, 3-phenylquinoline, Luotonin A, and harmine. These diverse chemical constituents contribute to the complex pharmacological profile of Peganum nigellas-trum. Research suggests that these specific quinoline derivatives and alkaloids may possess significant bioactive properties. For instance, the presence of Luotonin A and harmine often relates to neuroprotective or antimicrobial activities. Understanding these molecular structures helps scientists explore the therapeutic potential of the plant in modern medicine.

According to a study published by "Journal of the American Chemical Society", Peganum nigellastrum contains a pyrroloquinazolinoquinoline alkaloid known as luotonin A. This isolated compound has previously shown cytotoxicity against the murine leukemia P-388 cell line. Luotonin A stabilizes the human DNA topoisomerase I-DNA covalent binary complex. It also mediated topoisomerase I-dependent cytotoxicity toward Saccharomyces cerevisiae that lacks yeast topoisomerase I but harbors a plasmid with the human topoisomerase I gene under the control of a galactose promoter.

What side effects this plant can have?

This plant can cause adverse health effects including gastrointestinal distress such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and stomach pain. Some individuals may also experience topical skin irritation or neurological symptoms like dizziness. At a metabolic level, the plant has been linked to the risk of hypoglycemia. Furthermore, it may induce uterine contractions, which presents a significant concern for pregnant women.
