Sedum emarginatum

What's the taxonomical classification of Sedum emarginatum?

Sedum emarginatum belongs to the kingdom Plantae and is classified under the phylum Streptophyta. Within the class Equisetopsida, it is situated in the subclass Magnoliidae and falls under the order Saxifragales. This plant is a member of the family Crassulaceae, specifically placed within the genus Sedum, and is identified by the specific epithet emarginatum.

What are the morphological characteristics of this plant?

Sedum emarginatum has succulent, cylindrical to slightly flattened leaves that are typically bright green and arranged in a rosette or along creeping stems. These fleshy leaves often feature a slightly notched or emarginate tip, which is a defining characteristic of the species. The plant produces small, star-shaped flowers that transition from yellowish-green to pale pink or white depending on the specific variety. Its stems are often somewhat woody at the base and can spread across the ground to form dense mats. This low-growing perennial is well-adapted to rocky environments due to its water-storing tissues.

How is this plant cultivated?

This plant requires full sun exposure and well-draining, gritty soil to prevent root rot caused by excessive moisture.

It thrives in rocky crevices or containers where water can escape quickly between waterings. You should avoid overwatering, as this succulent is highly drought-tolerant and prefers to dry out completely in its growing medium. During the winter months, providing a period of dormancy in cooler temperatures helps maintain its natural growth cycle.

Regular maintenance involves removing dead foliage to encourage fresh, colorful growth in the spring.

According to a study published by "Journal of chromatographic science", the crude medicine Sedi Emarginati Herba is derived from Sedum emarginatum Migo. A high-performance liquid chromatography method was established to quantify quercetin and kaempferol in this plant using a YMC-pack ODS-A column measuring 250 x 4.6 mm with a 5 µm particle size. The process utilized a methanol-0.4% phosphoric acid mobile phase at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min at 35°C and a detection wavelength of 360 nm. Calibration curves were linear for quercetin over the range of 0.01-0.62 µg and for kaempferol over the range of 0.02-0.78 µg. Average recoveries for these compounds were 99.72% with a 1.63% RSD for quercetin and 99.50% with a 1.16% RSD for kaempferol. The best harvest season for this species is the full-bloom period between the end of April and the beginning of May.

What side effects this plant can have?

This plant can cause skin irritation, allergic reactions, and gastrointestinal distress if ingested. Contact with the leaves or stems may lead to redness, itching, or a localized rash on sensitive skin. Some individuals might experience more severe allergic responses such as swelling or hives upon exposure. If the plant is consumed, it can trigger nausea, vomiting, or stomach cramps. There is currently limited scientific research regarding the long-term toxicological impact of this species on humans.
