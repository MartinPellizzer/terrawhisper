Common St. John's wort

Hypericum perforatum · Accepted scientific name

Scientific literature: Very Extensive (2497 studies) · ★★★★★

Common St. John's wort, scientifically known as Hypericum perforatum, is a perennial flowering plant renowned for its medicinal properties. Primarily utilized in herbal medicine to alleviate mild-to-moderate depression, it contains bioactive compounds like hypericin. This versatile herb is widely studied for its therapeutic potential in supporting mental well-being.

Hypericum perforatum exhibits a highly versatile ecological profile, thriving across a broad spectrum of environments ranging from low-lying coastal areas to high-altitude alpine zones. Its elevational range is extensive, spanning from sea level (0 m AMSL) up to a maximum altitude of 3600 m AMSL. This adaptability allows the species to colonize a diverse array of habitats, including crop fields, forests, hills, meadows, mountains, roadsides, shrubberies, steppes, and stony places. The life history of Hypericum perforatum is characterized by its classification as a perennial plant, allowing it to persist through multiple growing seasons. Functionally, it operates as a hemicryptophyte, a life form where the perennating buds are located at the soil surface, protected by leaf litter or debris during unfavorable conditions. The species exhibits a deciduous nature, shedding its foliage annually as it transitions through its seasonal cycles. This combination of a perennial lifecycle, a hemicryptophyte growth habit, and deciduous phenology enables the plant to effectively navigate varying environmental shifts and re-establish its vegetative presence each spring.

Reproduction

The reproduction of Hypericum perforatum is primarily achieved through sexual means, utilizing bisexual flowers that facilitate the production of offspring. The flowering period typically commences in June and extends through August, featuring distinctive yellow flowers that range in length from 0.9 cm to 1.5 cm. These flowers exhibit a biotic pollination syndrome, specifically relying on insects such as bees to facilitate pollen transfer, though self-fertilization is also present. Following successful pollination, the plant develops dry, red capsules as its fruit type. These capsules are dehiscent and vary in size, with a mean length of 0.7 cm (ranging from 0.5 cm to 0.9 cm) and a mean width of 0.7 cm. Within these capsules, the plant produces a high volume of seeds, often exceeding 1,000 seeds per fruit. The individual seeds are quite small, characterized by a consistent length of 1.1 mm and a volume of approximately 0.13 mm³, with a mean mass of 0.000162254 g (ranging from 3.7e-05 g to 0.001 g). Dispersal is categorized as anemochorous, meaning the seeds are primarily spread by the wind. Notably, asexual reproduction is absent in this species.
