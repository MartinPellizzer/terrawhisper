tea plant

Camellia sinensis · Accepted scientific name

Scientific literature: Very Extensive (2678 studies) · ★★★★★

Tea plant, scientifically known as Camellia sinensis, is a versatile evergreen shrub celebrated globally for its medicinal and therapeutic properties. Rich in polyphenols and antioxidants, it serves as the primary source of tea. Beyond its calming ritual, it offers cognitive support, metabolic benefits, and essential cardiovascular protection for health.

Distribution

This plant exhibits a diverse geographical distribution across several key regions in Africa and the Indian Ocean. In West Tropical Africa, it can be found within the borders of Guinea. Its range extends to West-Central Tropical Africa, specifically around the Gulf of Guinea Islands. Moving toward the northeast, the species is also located in Ethiopia. Finally, it inhabits parts of the Western Indian Ocean, including the islands of Mauritius and Réunion.

Life history

The life history of Camellia sinensis is characterized by its nature as a perennial species, allowing it to persist and produce biomass over many successive growing seasons. As a phanerophyte, the plant maintains its photosynthetic organs on woody stems that elevate the foliage above the ground level, a trait further categorized by its specific classification as a phanerophyte in terms of its structural growth habit. Throughout its existence, it maintains an evergreen deciduousness, ensuring that its foliage remains functional and present year-round rather than shedding leaves seasonally.

Morphology

The morphology of Camellia sinensis is characterized by its woody nature and versatile growth forms, manifesting as both a shrub and a tree. As a self-supporting plant, it does not function as a climber or a vine, instead maintaining a sturdy, independent structure. It is a strictly terrestrial species, lacking any aquatic, semiaquatic, or epiphytic tendencies, and grows as an independent organism rather than a parasite. In terms of physical stature, the plant can reach a significant size, with a maximum plant height of 10 m and a mean height typically reaching around 10 m under optimal conditions.

Physiology

The physiology of Camellia sinensis is characterized by a C3 photosynthetic pathway, which dictates its metabolic efficiency and carbon fixation processes. Morphologically, the plant produces leaves with an elliptic form and distinctive toothed margins, providing the structural framework necessary for efficient light interception and gas exchange. The foliage is smooth in terms of defensive structures, as it possesses no leaf thorns and no leaf spines. Furthermore, the plant does not function as a nitrogen fixer, meaning it relies on the availability of nitrogen within the soil substrate to support its growth and the synthesis of secondary metabolites.

Reproduction

The reproduction of Camellia sinensis is characterized by a specific flowering period and a reliance on biotic agents for successful fertilization. The flowering cycle typically begins in August and concludes in September, although the timing can be variable. During this stage, the plant utilizes a biotic pollination syndrome, specifically relying on insects, with bees playing a prominent role in the pollination process. Notably, self-fertilization is absent in this species, necessitating these external pollinators to facilitate genetic exchange. Following successful pollination, the plant produces seeds that exhibit a range of masses; while the mean seed mass is approximately 1.5151125 g, individual seeds can vary significantly, with a minimum mass of 0.54945 g and a maximum mass reaching up to 2.041 g.
