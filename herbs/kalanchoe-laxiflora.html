Physiology

The physiology of Kalanchoe laxiflora is fundamentally defined by its specialized water-management strategies, most notably its pronounced succulence. As a succulent, the plant possesses thickened, fleshy tissues within its leaves and stems that serve as primary reservoirs for water storage, allowing it to maintain cellular turgidity and metabolic function during periods of prolonged drought. This morphological adaptation is closely linked to its specialized photosynthetic pathway, Crassulacean Acid Metabolism (CAM). In this physiological process, the plant minimizes transpirational water loss by keeping its stomata closed during the heat of the day and opening them only at night to collect carbon dioxide. This nocturnal CO2 uptake is subsequently stored as malic acid, which is then decarboxylated during the daylight hours to fuel the Calvin cycle. By decoupling gas exchange from solar radiation, Kalanchoe laxiflora exhibits high water-use efficiency, enabling it to thrive in arid or semi-arid environments where water availability is inconsistent.
