Salvia coccinea

What's the taxonomical classification of Salvia coccinea?

Salvia coccinea belongs to the kingdom Plantae and is classified within the phylum Streptophyta. At the class level, it is categorized as Equisetopsida, falling under the subclass Magnoliidae. This plant is further organized within the order Lamiales and belongs to the family Lamiaceae. Within this family, it is placed in the genus Salvia, and its specific biological identity is defined by the species name coccinea.

What are the morphological characteristics of this plant?

Salvía coccinea has erect, square-shaped stems that can reach heights of up to 3 feet and are covered in fine, glandular hairs. The leaves are oppositely arranged, lanceolate in shape, and possess serrated margins with a textured, rugose surface. Its inflorescence consists of dense terminal racemes bearing tubular, bright scarlet flowers that are characteristic of the mint family. Each blossom features a two-lipped structure designed to facilitate pollination by hummingbirds and insects. The plant typically develops a woody base as it matures, providing structural support for its upright growth habit.

What is the geographical distribution of this plant?

This plant is native to the southwestern United States and northwestern Mexico, specifically spanning regions within Arizona, New Mexico, Texas, and several Mexican states. It thrives in arid and semi-arid environments ranging from low-elevation deserts to higher montane scrublands. The species is most commonly found in rocky slopes, canyon bottoms, and open woodlands where well-drained soil is available. Its range is characterized by a preference for sun-drenched habitats typical of the Sonoran and Chihuahuan desert ecosystems. Because it is adapted to dry climates, it maintains a widespread presence across these specific subtropical landscapes.

How is this plant cultivated?

This plant requires full sun and well-draining, sandy or loamy soil to thrive in USDA hardiness zones 7 through 10.

You should provide regular watering during the establishment phase, but allow the soil to dry out significantly between waterings once the plant is mature. It performs best when planted in locations with excellent air circulation to prevent fungal issues. Because it is a short-lived perennial, you can propagate it easily through stem cuttings taken in late spring.

Deadheading the scarlet flowers throughout the summer will encourage continuous blooms and attract pollinators like hummingbirds.

According to a study published by "Acta histochemica", Salvia coccinea leaf aqueous extract (AESL) is used to treat inflammatory disorders like diabetes. In studies involving THP-1 cells and STZ-induced diabetic Wistar rats, AESL was administered orally for 14 days. The plant shows potential for controlling blood glucose and protecting against damage caused by hyperglycemia-induced inflammation. AESL treatment reduces ROS production and NF-κB dependent proinflammatory gene expression in HG treated THP-1 cells. Furthermore, oral administration of AESL inhibits increases in serum lipid peroxidation, aspartate transaminase, alanine transaminase, and lactate dehydrogenase in diabetic rats.

What are the herbal preparations of this plant?

This plant is Salvia coccineas, which can be processed into medicinal herbal preparations such as aqueous extracts of the leaf and methanol extracts. The aqueous extract is typically prepared by steeping the leaves in water to isolate water-soluble bioactive compounds. In contrast, methanol extracts utilize alcohol to draw out a broader range of lipophilic phytochemicals from the plant material. These different preparation methods allow researchers to target specific chemical profiles for therapeutic study. Such extracts are often analyzed to determine their antioxidant or antimicrobial properties.

According to a study published by "Acta histochemica", the Aqueous Extract of S. coccinea leaf (AESL) was administered orally via intragastric tube for 14 days. This preparation was used to evaluate antioxidant effects in HG treated THP-1 cells and streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic Wistar rats. AESL treatment reduced HG induced increases in ROS production and NF-κB dependent proinflammatory gene expression. In diabetic rats, oral administration of AESL inhibited STZ-induced increases in serum lipid peroxidation, aspartate transaminase, alanine transaminase, and lactate dehydrogenase. The treatment also caused a significant increase in the activity of superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione peroxidase, alongside a reduced level of glutathione. Results demonstrate that AESL is useful in controlling blood glucose and possesses antioxidant potential to protect against hyperglycemia-induced inflammation.
