Buddleja davidii, scientifically known as Buddlejia davidii, is a vigorous deciduous shrub celebrated for its fragrant, cone-shaped flower clusters. Often called the "Butterfly Bush," it serves as a vital nectar source for pollinators. Beyond its ornamental beauty, various species possess traditional medicinal properties used in diverse ethnobotanical practices.

Reproduction

The reproduction of Buddleja davidii is characterized by a sexual process, as asexual reproduction is absent. The flowering period typically begins in June and extends through October, facilitating a long reproductive season. The plant relies on biotic pollination syndromes, specifically utilizing insects, with a notable affinity for butterflies to facilitate pollen transfer; notably, self-fertilization is absent in this species. Following successful pollination, the plant develops fruit in the form of a capsule. These capsules are dehiscent, meaning they split open to release the seeds. The fruits vary in size, with a mean length of 0.75 cm, ranging from a minimum of 0.5 cm to a maximum of 0.9 cm. The seeds themselves are extremely lightweight, with a mean mass of 7.85e-05 g and a range between 1e-04 g and 1e-04 g. For dispersal, the plant employs an anemochorous syndrome, utilizing the wind to transport its tiny seeds to new locations.
