Foxglove

Digitalis purpurea · Accepted scientific name

Scientific literature: Very Extensive (492 studies) · ★★★★★

Foxglove, scientifically known as Digitalis purpurea, is a striking perennial herb celebrated for its bell-shaped, violet-to-pink blooms. While visually enchanting, this plant contains potent cardiac glycosides. Historically used to treat heart ailments, it must be handled with extreme caution, as its chemical compounds are highly toxic if ingested.

Reproduction

The reproduction of Digitalis purpurea is primarily sexual, characterized by bisexual flowers that facilitate genetic diversity. The flowering period typically commences in June and concludes in July, featuring prominent flowers that range in length from 4 cm to 5 cm and display a red coloration. These flowers rely on biotic pollination, specifically utilizing an insect-based syndrome where bees serve as key pollinators. While the plant is capable of sexual reproduction, self-fertilization is noted to be absent, and asexual reproduction is also absent, with the plant functioning as a monocarp. Following successful pollination, the plant develops dry, dehiscence-type capsules as its fruit type. These capsules vary in size, with a mean length of 1.35 cm (ranging from 0.9 cm to 1.5 cm) and a width between 0.6 cm and 1 cm. Each fruit is highly prolific, containing more than 1,000 seeds. The individual seeds are minute, possessing a mean mass of approximately 8.78e-05 g (ranging from 7e-05 g to 1e-04 g) and a consistent volume of 0.073 mm³. Dispersal of these seeds is achieved through both anemochorous (wind) and anthropochorous (human-mediated) syndromes.
