Dogís ears, scientifically known as Cotyledon orbiculata, is a striking succulent native to South Africa. Characterized by its thick, fleshy, paddle-shaped leaves, this resilient plant is prized in medicinal traditions for its healing properties and as a unique ornamental specimen in modern succulent gardens and landscaping.

The physiology of Cotyledon orbiculata is characterized by specialized adaptations for water conservation and efficient resource management in arid environments. It utilizes the Crassulacean Acid Metabolism (CAM) photosynthetic pathway, an adaptation that allows the plant to fix carbon dioxide at night to minimize transpirational water loss during the heat of the day. This metabolic strategy is closely integrated with its high degree of succulence, where specialized parenchymatous tissues serve as reservoirs for water storage. The plant's foliage is morphologically optimized for this storage, featuring obovate-shaped leaves that are arranged in an opposite pattern along the stem. These leaves exhibit significant morphological plasticity in size, with lengths ranging from a minimum of 3 cm to a maximum of 12 cm, and widths varying from 1 cm to a mean and maximum of 9 cm. This structural configuration supports the plant's physiological ability to maintain hydration and metabolic activity under fluctuating environmental stressors.

Reproduction

The reproduction of Cotyledon orbiculata is characterized by a flowering period that typically begins in December and concludes in March, though the timing can be variable throughout the year. The plant produces distinct orange flowers that vary in size, with a length ranging from 1.5 cm to 3 cm and a width between 0.6 cm and 1 cm. Following pollination, the plant develops fruit in the form of a follicle, which reaches an average length of 2.5 cm. The seeds produced are quite small, maintaining a consistent length of approximately 0.7 mm. In terms of mass, the seeds are extremely lightweight, with a mean mass of 1.75e-05 g, ranging from a minimum of 1e-05 g to a maximum of 6e-05 g. Dispersal of these seeds is achieved through an anemochorous syndrome, meaning they are primarily dispersed by the wind.
