Tall morning-glory, scientifically known as Ipomoea purpurea, is a vigorous climbing vine celebrated for its vibrant, trumpet-shaped blooms. Beyond its ornamental beauty, this plant holds historical significance in traditional medicine, often utilized for its diverse pharmacological properties, including its potential sedative, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial effects in various cultures.

The ecology of Ipomoea purpurea is characterized by its remarkable adaptability to diverse environmental conditions and a wide altitudinal distribution. It thrives across a broad elevational range, spanning from sea level (0 m AMSL) up to altitudes of 2800 m AMSL. Reproduction

The reproduction of Ipomoea purpurea is characterized by a distinct flowering season that typically commences in August and extends through November, though the timing can be variable. The plant produces striking blue flowers that range in length from 4 cm to a maximum of 5 cm. Following pollination, the plant develops fruit in the form of a capsule, which measures between 0.9 cm and 1.5 cm in width and between 1 cm and 1.2 cm in length. These capsules are dehiscence-type fruits, meaning they split open to release their contents. Each fruit typically contains a yield of 1 to 10 seeds. The seeds themselves are uniform in length, averaging and ranging consistently at 7 mm. In terms of mass, the seeds vary from a minimum of 0.02182 g to a maximum of 0.04 g, with a mean seed mass of approximately 0.030205 g. The dispersal syndrome for this species is classified as unspecialized, allowing the seeds to propagate through various non-specific environmental mechanisms.
